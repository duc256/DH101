## Process Documentation
For this project, I used Suno to explore how AI interprets shifting emotional contexts within the rigid stylistic framework of Neo-Soul and Alternative R&B. I set up the generator to produce tracks heavily inspired by artists like Daniel Caesar, establishing a strict baseline prompt that requested "warm electric piano, smooth deep bass," and "intimate breathy male vocals."

To maintain systematic variation, I kept those core tags constant while strictly altering the tempo, mood, and atmospheric textures across 8 different prompt iterations. 

During the curation phase, my primary criteria were conceptual fit and aesthetic quality. The curation process required heavy filtering. I rejected several tracks (specifically variations 1, 3, and 7) because they felt "too smooth" or generic, blurring into a standard lo-fi chill vibe rather than distinct R&B. I also rejected variation 4 because the AI interpreted the acoustic prompt as a mellow ballad, completely missing the intended groove. Filtering out these generic, mathematically safe outputs proved that generating a high volume of material is absolutely necessary to find genuinely compelling algorithmic music.

## Artist Statement
When an AI can generate endlessly, creativity shifts from the act of mechanical production to the act of editorial taste. In this process, the human element existed strictly at the boundaries: first in the conceptual engineering of the R&B prompts, and finally in the strict curation of the outputs.

My creative decisions occurred when I specifically chose to warm, traditional instruments with varying atmospheric constraints, and when I brutally cut tracks that felt too mellow or structurally generic. If someone listened to my final three tracks without explanation, they might not know a human selected them, but they would sense a coherent, intimate aesthetic that a machine, lacking intent, cannot formulate on its own.

"Pure" AI creativity is an oxymoron. It is a slot machine of statistical probabilities matching patterns. "Pure" human creativity involves starting from nothing and building with emotional intent. My work falls squarely in the middle: it is machine-executed but human-directed.

During the process, I acutely felt the "exhaustion of novelty." I knew it was time to stop generating when prompts 1, 3, and 7 all started sounding identical—a wash of "too chill" lo-fi beats. The magic of instant music faded, and the variations started blurring together. True curation requires accepting the imperfections of the system and knowing when to walk away with the best takes.
