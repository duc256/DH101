<section class="section">
  <h2>My basic rule</h2>
  <p class="big-quote">
    I used AI as a drafting, generating, brainstorming, and revision tool. I did not treat AI as the final author of my work.
  </p>

  <p>
    In most projects, AI helped me start faster or test more possibilities. However, I made the final decisions about what counted as successful, what needed to be changed, and how the project connected to the course. This distinction matters because AI can produce fluent or polished output without understanding my background, cultural context, ethical concerns, or final purpose.
  </p>
</section>

<section class="section">
  <h2>Concrete examples of AI output I included or changed</h2>

  <div class="timeline">
    <div class="timeline-item">
      <h3>Example 1 — AI selfie</h3>
      <p>
        AI generated a photorealistic image based on my prompt about appearance, glasses, hair, and facial features. I did not treat the first image as an accurate version of myself. I chose one output, then edited a second version in Canva by adding labels, arrows, and visual distortion. My human work was to make the gap between “me” and the AI version visible.
      </p>
    </div>

    <div class="timeline-item">
      <h3>Example 2 — Tết comic</h3>
      <p>
        AI helped generate image drafts for a comic about Lunar New Year in Vietnam. I changed the story flow, selected character emotions, revised dialogue, and fixed the meaning of the sequence. The AI could make images, but it did not automatically understand the cultural lesson or the emotional timing of the apology.
      </p>
    </div>

    <div class="timeline-item">
      <h3>Example 3 — Suno music generator</h3>
      <p>
        Suno generated multiple tracks from my R&B/neo-soul prompt variations. I rejected several outputs because they sounded too generic or too similar. My role was not only prompting, but also curation: deciding which tracks fit the aesthetic and which ones failed.
      </p>
    </div>

    <div class="timeline-item">
      <h3>Example 4 — Writing revision</h3>
      <p>
        I used ChatGPT to help organize some reflections and improve clarity. I revised wording, checked whether the claims matched my actual project, and removed phrasing that sounded too generic or not like me.
      </p>
    </div>
  </div>
</section>

<section class="section">
  <h2>What I verified or corrected</h2>
  <ul>
    <li>I checked whether generated images matched the cultural or emotional meaning I wanted.</li>
    <li>I compared AI interpretation with visible evidence, especially in the distant-reading project.</li>
    <li>I rejected outputs that were polished but too generic.</li>
    <li>I added attribution boxes to explain the difference between AI contribution and human contribution.</li>
    <li>I kept source-based projects connected to readings, data, and my own analysis instead of relying only on AI summaries.</li>
  </ul>
</section>

<section class="section">
  <h2>Limits, bias, and ethics</h2>
  <p>
    The biggest limit of AI in my work was not that it made obvious mistakes every time. The more dangerous issue was that it could produce smooth answers that looked complete while hiding uncertainty, bias, or missing context. In the selfie and comic projects, AI tended to simplify identity and culture into readable visual patterns. In the mapping, labor, and ecology projects, I learned that AI also depends on infrastructures and workers that are often invisible to users.
  </p>

  <p>
    This is why transparency matters. I want viewers to know where AI helped and where I made decisions. I also want the site to avoid presenting AI output as neutral or magical. My final position is that AI can support creative work, but responsible making requires human judgment, citation, correction, and accountability.
  </p>
</section>
