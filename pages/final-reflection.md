  <h2>Final reflection</h2>

  <p>
    At the beginning of the semester, I thought the main question about AI and making was simple: does AI make creativity easier or does it replace human creativity? After working through the projects in this portfolio, I no longer think that is the strongest question. A better question is: when AI helps us make something, what parts of the process become easier to see, and what parts become easier to ignore?
  </p>

  <p>
    My answer is that being human in the age of AI means staying responsible for meaning, context, and consequences when machines make production feel fast. AI can generate images, organize text, make songs, suggest code, and create visual styles. But the human role does not disappear. It moves into prompting, selecting, rejecting, revising, checking, contextualizing, and explaining why something matters. In this portfolio, I learned that human creativity is not only the act of producing an object. It is also the act of making judgment.
  </p>

  <p>
    My AI selfie project made this clear first. The generated image looked close enough to me that it felt familiar, but it also simplified me. It made my face smoother, calmer, and more generic. This connects to the question of authenticity. AI did not simply copy my identity; it interpreted my prompt through patterns it had already learned. That made me think about identity as something that can be flattened by a system. The second edited selfie was important because I added labels and distortion to show the difference between my lived self and the AI-readable version of me. In that project, being human meant noticing the gap and choosing not to hide it.
  </p>

  <p>
    The Tết comic pushed this lesson further. AI could generate panels, but it could not fully understand why the story mattered culturally. The comic was about a child learning respect during Lunar New Year, and the emotional timing had to feel right. Small visual mistakes, like repeated characters, inconsistent clothing, or confusing speech bubbles, changed the meaning of the story. That project taught me that AI can imitate cultural images, but cultural meaning still requires human care. I had to decide what felt respectful, what felt confusing, and what the lesson should be. In this case, the human role was not only creative control. It was cultural responsibility.
  </p>

  <p>
    D’Ignazio and Klein’s <em>Data Feminism</em> helped me understand why these choices matter beyond my own projects. Their work asks us to pay attention to power: who is represented, who is missing, who benefits, and who does the work. I used that idea most directly in my map of AI worlds. Instead of mapping AI as an abstract “cloud,” I mapped headquarters, data centers, and invisible labor sites. That changed my view of AI. The system is not floating above the world. It is located in the world. It depends on land, energy, corporate decisions, workers, and communities. What was missing from the map also mattered. It was much easier to find polished corporate information than detailed information about subcontracted labor or local environmental burdens. That missingness became part of the argument.
  </p>

  <p>
    Drucker’s argument that visualizations are interpretations, not neutral facts, also shaped my network project. My personal digital ecosystem looked objective because it had nodes, edges, and categories, but I made many choices about what counted as a node and what counted as a connection. Google and Apple became central hubs because many of my apps and services connect back to them. But that structure was still only one version of the truth. If I had centered data brokers, content moderators, or data centers, the network would have looked different. This helped me understand that visual making is powerful because it can reveal relationships, but it can also frame reality in a limited way.
  </p>

  <p>
    The labor project changed my thinking the most emotionally. Before that assignment, I often thought of AI as a technical achievement. After researching data labeling and ghost work, I started seeing AI as a labor system. Gray and Suri’s idea of “ghost work” helped me understand that many systems look automated because human workers are hidden behind the interface. Hicks’s article on technology and exclusion also helped me see that systems often present themselves as neutral while carrying assumptions about people and social order. My infographic tried to make hidden workers visible because AI is not only built by engineers or models. It is also built by people who label, filter, moderate, and clean data, often with little recognition.
  </p>

  <p>
    The environmental visualization added another layer. A single AI prompt can feel small, but AI at scale requires electricity, water, cooling, hardware, and data-center expansion. Crawford’s work on AI as an extractive system helped me think about this problem. AI is not only a software tool. It is connected to material resources and unequal costs. My visualization compared different scales of AI impact because I wanted to show how the user experience can hide the infrastructure behind it. The interface feels clean, but the system is not weightless.
  </p>

  <p>
    By the time I made the speculative housing artifact, my thinking had shifted from “What can AI create?” to “What systems can AI strengthen?” The automated eviction notice from 2046 imagined a future where housing decisions are made through behavioral data and algorithmic compliance scores. What made that future disturbing was not only the technology. It was the power structure behind it. The artifact suggested that AI does not need to “take over” in a dramatic way. It can quietly expand systems that already exist: surveillance, landlord control, unequal access to appeal, and automated decision-making.
  </p>

  <p>
    This is where my final answer comes from. To be human in the age of AI is not to reject AI completely, and it is not to accept it passively. It is to remain aware of what AI can and cannot do. AI can help produce drafts, images, sounds, patterns, and structures. But it does not live with the consequences of those choices. It does not know what it means to be misrepresented, to have a cultural story flattened, to work invisibly, to live near a data center, or to be judged by an automated system. Humans do.
  </p>

  <p>
    Therefore, human making in the age of AI must include responsibility. It must include attribution, correction, refusal, and care. My work this semester is not perfect, but it shows a real shift in my thinking. I started by asking whether AI could help me make things. I ended by asking what kind of world is being made through AI, who gets to shape it, and who pays the cost. That question feels more honest, and it is the question I want this portfolio to leave with the viewer.
  </p>
