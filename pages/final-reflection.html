What It Means to Be Human in the Age of AI

My final argument: AI does not erase the human role in making. It changes where the human role appears.

Final reflection

At the beginning of the semester, I thought the main question about AI and making was simple: does AI make creativity easier, or does it replace human creativity? After working through the projects in this portfolio, I no longer think that is the strongest question. A better question is: when AI helps us make something, what parts of the process become easier to see, and what parts become easier to ignore?

My answer is that being human in the age of AI means staying responsible for meaning, context, and consequences when machines make production feel fast. AI can generate images, organize text, make songs, suggest code, and create visual styles. But the human role does not disappear. It moves into prompting, selecting, rejecting, revising, checking, contextualizing, and explaining why something matters.

My AI selfie project made this clear first. The generated image looked familiar, but it also simplified me. It made my face smoother, calmer, and more generic. That made me think about identity as something that can be flattened by a system. In that project, being human meant noticing the gap between my lived self and the AI-readable version of me.

The Tết comic pushed this lesson further. AI could generate panels, but it could not fully understand why the story mattered culturally. The comic was about respect during Lunar New Year, and the emotional timing had to feel right. AI could imitate cultural images, but cultural meaning still required human care.

D’Ignazio and Klein’s Data Feminism helped me understand why these choices matter beyond my own projects. Their work asks us to pay attention to power: who is represented, who is missing, who benefits, and who does the work. I used that idea in my map of AI worlds. Instead of mapping AI as an abstract cloud, I mapped headquarters, data centers, and invisible labor sites. This made AI feel physical and political.

Drucker’s argument that visualizations are interpretations, not neutral facts, also shaped my network project. My personal digital ecosystem looked objective because it had nodes and edges, but I made choices about what counted as a node and what counted as a connection. This helped me understand that visual making can reveal relationships, but it can also frame reality in a limited way.

The labor project changed my thinking the most emotionally. Gray and Suri’s idea of ghost work helped me understand that many systems look automated because human workers are hidden behind the interface. My infographic tried to make hidden workers visible because AI is not only built by engineers or models. It is also built by people who label, filter, moderate, and clean data.

The environmental visualization added another layer. Crawford’s work on AI as an extractive system helped me think about AI as material infrastructure connected to energy, water, hardware, and environmental cost. The interface feels clean, but the system is not weightless.

By the end of the semester, my thinking had shifted from “What can AI create?” to “What systems can AI strengthen?” To be human in the age of AI is not to reject AI completely, and it is not to accept it passively. It is to remain aware of what AI can and cannot do. AI can help produce drafts, images, sounds, and patterns. But it does not live with the consequences of those choices. Humans do.

  • Gray and Suri, Ghost Work
